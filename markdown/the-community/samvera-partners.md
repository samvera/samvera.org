## When you fund Samvera, you help steer it

Most of the infrastructure your library runs on is decided somewhere you don't sit. A vendor sets the roadmap, the price, and the renewal, and you adjust to it. If you run shared infrastructure for member institutions, you feel this twice: once for your own budget, and again every time you have to explain a cost increase to a board that approved a different number.

Samvera works the other way. The software that runs your repositories (Hyrax, Hyku, and the Avalon media system) is open source and free to download, deploy, and run on your own terms. What sustains it is the community of institutions that fund and build it together. Funding Samvera is closer to co-investing in shared infrastructure you help direct, alongside peers who depend on the same software you do, than it is to buying a license.

A provost or a board will ask why pay for software that's free. The code being free is what protects you, and the community is what keeps that code maintained, current, and moving in a direction your institution had a hand in setting. Those two facts answer the question together, and they're both public, so a budget-holder can check them before committing a dollar.

There's also a student-success angle a provost will recognize. In a 2024 study of small and mid-sized US colleges, institutions with above-average student retention were more likely than expected to have an institutional repository that includes student work ([Szpunar and Bradley, 2024](https://doi.org/10.18438/eblip30495)). The authors report an association, and it gives a director something concrete: a repository that showcases student scholarship has a place in the retention story your library tells.

### What your funding buys: a seat where the roadmap is set

Samvera is governed by the institutions that fund it.

Samvera Partners elect a nine-member Board of Directors. Candidates are nominated and supported by a Partner institution, terms run three years (renewable once, to a maximum of six), and three of the nine seats are elected each year, so the membership renews the board on a regular cadence. Partners hold the right to nominate candidates and to vote on those elections and on other matters that come before the community.

Direction is set in the open, in groups Partners help run. The Roadmaps Alignment Group coordinates where the software is headed across Hyrax, Hyku, and Avalon. The Core Components Maintenance Group keeps the shared core components healthy and actively maintained. Interest and Working Groups carry the specific work, and they're open to the people who do it day to day. A repository administrator on your staff can help shape a feature your patrons will use, and a director can sit on the body that prioritizes it.

The structure is documented and public. Samvera runs its legal, accounting, and administrative back-end through OASIS Open's Foundation-as-a-Service program, which lets the community put its energy into the software rather than into running a nonprofit while keeping its own governance and rules. The [Samvera Bylaws](https://samvera.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/samvera/pages/1387986974/Samvera+Bylaws) set out how the board is elected, how Partners vote, and how decisions are made. You can read the bylaws and the [current Partner roster](https://samvera.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/samvera/pages/1872461833/About+Samvera+Partners) before you reach out, and self-qualify on your own time.

### How consortial cost-sharing works

If you run shared infrastructure for member institutions, this is built for you.

Hyku was built for multi-tenancy from the ground up. One platform runs many fully branded sites, each tenant with its own collections, access controls, and configuration. The cost of the infrastructure and the development that improves it is pooled across everyone the platform serves, so each institution pays a share of pooled infrastructure rather than carrying the full cost of standing up and maintaining a repository alone. As more institutions join, the per-institution share goes down.

For a consortium director, that changes the recruitment math. A small library that could never justify its own repository can join a shared instance and get a real one, branded as theirs, on standards-based infrastructure. The operational load of servers, patching, and upgrades sits with the consortium rather than with each member.

It also changes the conversation with your board. The community sets its funding levels and reinvests them in the software everyone runs. The Community Manager can walk a budget-holder through the current levels before any deeper conversation, so the number you take to your board is one the community set in the open, not a vendor's annual increase you find out about after the fact.

### What protects you if you ever want to leave

A funder should ask what happens if the relationship ends. The honest answer is that you keep control the whole way through.

The software is licensed under Apache 2.0. You can run and modify it, and host it yourself or with any provider you choose. Your description and metadata live in documented, interoperable open standards (Dublin Core, IIIF, OAI-PMH), so your data travels in formats anyone can read. Hyku's Valkyrie data layer lets you choose your metadata backend and migrate between backends by reading from one and writing to another, and it ships with an S3-compatible storage option you can point at a cloud provider or your own object store. None of this depends on a single vendor's goodwill to keep running.

That's the structural version of the control a library wants over its own infrastructure. You may never want to leave, and the point is that you always could, on your own terms, with your data intact.

### The honest part: the community is smaller, and that cuts both ways

Samvera is a focused community, smaller than a mass-market platform with thousands of installs. That means a smaller pool of third-party add-ons and a documentation set still catching up in places, and you should weigh that going in.

The same size is why your voice carries. In a community this size, a Partner that funds the work is a real participant in setting direction. The protections above are what make the size safe to bet on: the code is Apache-licensed and deployable by any competent Ruby on Rails team, your data moves in open standards, and the roadmap is set by the institutions that fund it rather than by one company's commercial priorities. You're funding infrastructure you can read, run, and steer, which is a different kind of security than a vendor's promise to stay in business.

### How to become a Partner

Partnership is how an institution earns standing in the community. Partners pay annual dues and contribute in one or more of several ways: code, analysis, design, documentation, communication and presentations, marketing, support, funding, or participation in the groups that do the work.

The path is deliberate. You take part in the community, an existing Partner or the Board nominates you, you write a short letter of intent describing what you'll contribute, and the Board votes you in. Once you're a Partner, you vote on board elections and community matters, you can nominate and hold board seats, you help set the roadmap, and you receive advance notice of security vulnerabilities through the Partners' channel ahead of public disclosure. For a consortium or an institution that wants a hand on the direction of the software it runs, this is the path to real standing.
